## 1. Interaction scores — the family of Wolff, Cardew, Zorn

*(His question, 2026-10-11: is there a common name for "this collection of interaction notation or algorithms"? The earlier
discussion: DEC-36b, 2026-10-06 — the three-body problem's gravity as "Christian Wolff-like contingencies".)*

### 1.1 The finding

**There is no one agreed name.** Four names are in use, each with its own writers:

| The name | Whose | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **contextual processes** · **people processes** | Michael Nyman (1974) | The oldest scholarly name; later writers cite it. Two of his five kinds of process (chance determination · people · contextual · repetition · electronic). |
| **cueing** (cueing strategies, cueing scores) | Wolff scholarship (Philip Thomas, Emily Payne); James Saunders ("rule-based cueing") | The working word for Wolff's notation. In this repo's notes his own word is "contingency". |
| **game pieces** | John Zorn, for his own (*Cobra*, *Hockey*, *Lacrosse*, *Xu Feng*) | The idea is usually traced to Xenakis (*Duel*, *Stratégie*), Wolff and Zorn; described as "controlled improvisation". |
| **rule-based compositions** · **group behaviours as music** · **behavioural-musical systems** | James Saunders (Open Scores Lab, Bath Spa University), 2015 … 2021 | THE ONE CURRENT BODY OF RESEARCH that treats the family as a field — with models from social psychology (Forsyth's group dynamics), decision theory (heuristics) and game studies. |

The broader umbrellas the family sits under: indeterminacy (of performance) · verbal or text scores · open scores.

**For the paper (the AI's reading, marked as such):** "contextual processes" (Nyman) is the citable historical name; Saunders's
framework is the citable current one. The neighbour field is the psychology of ensemble coordination (joint action; anticipation
and adaptation in synchronisation) — the book of 1.2's first two entries is exactly where the two fields meet, and this piece's
anticipation–reaction returns and its three-body section sit in that overlap.

### 1.2 The key texts

1. ✔ **James Saunders, "Group behaviours as music"**, in Renee Timmers, Freya Bailes and Helena Daffern (eds.), *Together in
   Music: Coordination, Expression, Participation*, Oxford University Press, 2021 (ISBN 9780198860761). — THE FRAMEWORK CHAPTER.
   Group behaviour as a compositional strategy: rules govern the interaction, decisions are enacted by the players' choices,
   values are suggested by the outcome; organised by Forsyth's five group processes (formative · influence · performance ·
   conflict · contextual); its terms "behavioural-musical systems", "decision-spaces", music-as-script (Cook); ties itself to
   Nyman's "people processes" and "contextual processes". Its examples: Zorn's *Cobra*, Oliveros's *Sonic Meditation VII*, and
   pieces by Porfiriadis, Steenberge, Sdraulig, Pocknee, Miller, Lely and Saunders himself. **Wolff and Cardew are NOT among its

## 2. The three-body problem — the physics behind section two

*(His asks, 2026-10-11: a lay summary of the physics for the performance instructions; "is the term chaos true?"; "if there are
actual names for the four categories, then let's use them".)*

### 2.1 What the physics calls his four phases

| His phase (the page) | The physics' name | Sure? |
|---|---|---|
| Far Apart | no standard name — the nearest is a **hierarchical** arrangement (a close pair and a distant third), not quite the same thing | ◐ |
| Approaching | no standard name | — |
| Close Pass | a **close encounter** (also "close approach"; all three at once a "triple close approach"; a long tangle with no two-body character is "interplay") | ✔ |
| Break and Rejoin | an **ejection** — one body separates from the pair that forms, slows, and RETURNS; if it never returns it is an **escape** | ✔ |

The whole cycle, in one source's words in paraphrase: chaotic evolution runs as a series of close triple encounters, between
which one body is temporarily ejected, stays bound, and comes back.

### 2.2 The terms on the instructions page

- **Deterministic chaos** — a system that follows exact laws (deterministic) and yet cannot be predicted FAR AHEAD: tiny
  differences at the start grow until a forecast fails. The defining property: sensitive dependence on initial conditions (the
  "butterfly effect"). First met in the three-body problem itself — Henri Poincaré, the 1880s. ✗ *From the AI's knowledge, not
  looked up; standard textbook matter — a source to add before the paper cites it.* The page's sentence leaves "far ahead" out,
  at his choice (RUNNING_LOG §474, the copy edit's number 10).
- **Two bodies are solved exactly** (the orbit is a clock); Halley's Comet, about every 76 years (74 … 79, because Jupiter and
  Saturn tug on it), is the lay example; an eclipse is NOT one — it needs the Sun, the Earth and the Moon. ✗ *the AI's knowledge.*
- **"No formula"** is true of the general case; a few special arrangements of three bodies have exact solutions. ✗ *the AI's knowledge.*

### 2.3 The sources

1. ✔ **Victor Szebehely, "Review of the dynamical aspects of triple systems"** (International Astronomical Union Colloquium) —
   the classification of three-body motions: interplay · triple close approach · ejection · revolution · equilibrium · periodic
   orbits · oscillation (bounded), and escape. Its classification of transient behaviour follows Szebehely (1971) and Agekian
   (1973). *(seen 2026-10-11 on Cambridge Core; the volume and year not noted — to complete.)*
   https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/B618313E3F703109805C73366C9018A4/S0252921100052593a.pdf/review-of-the-dynamical-aspects-of-triple-systems.pdf
2. ◐ Victor Szebehely, "Classification of the motions of three bodies in a plane", *Celestial Mechanics* 4, 1971, pp. 116–118 —
   cited by the review; not seen itself.
3. ✔ "When does a star cluster become a multiple star system? I. Lifetimes of equal-mass small-N systems" (arXiv 1603.07731) —
   the series of close triple encounters with temporary ejections between them. *(the authors and the journal not noted — to complete.)*

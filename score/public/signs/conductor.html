The conductor's view — working page

The players do not see what the electronics play back. A conductor's or presentation score may carry a hint of it. One section at a time.

On every frame the players' own signs — the yellow mic brick, the red badge, the pink arc — stand where the composer score has them or where nothing is decided yet. Their places are provisional. Look at the GREY marks: those are the hint.

What the electronics do, section by section

  1. 1 · The opening (0 … 37 s). Each player's short attack is recorded. From the second round on, every new attack is shadowed by that player's EARLIER attacks, transformed: first one of them, then two, three, four, five. They fall a little before, around or after the live note, never exactly with it, and the engine rolls their exact times at each performance.
  2. 2 · The three body problem (39 … 123 s). Three computer players join the five. They move through the same four states as the players — far apart, approaching, close pass, break and rejoin — listen to them, and decide each sound live, from the transformed attacks.
  3. 3 · Trills and petal hits (125 … 210 s). At the end of each trill another player plays one short note into an open microphone, and the electronics ring it on as a long resonant tail of about ten seconds. Thirty-four of them.
  4. 4 · The drones (211 … 415 s). Each player's held multiphonic is recorded three times. Each recording comes back stretched into drones of 10 to 40 seconds, up to five at once, until a long fade at the end.
  5. 5 · The beating section (423 … 668 s). The electronics hold a pure tone at the player's pitch. The player bends against it, so the pair beats. Thirty-five pairs.
  6. 6 · The strikes (not yet in the piece). The ensemble strikes. The electronics answer after the strike, with its rhythm transformed, on the recorded attacks.
